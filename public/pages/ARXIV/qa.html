Q & A


This page offers colleagues a glimpse into the human behind the work.

  • What's your story with coding?

    The first time I used a terminal, I was around 7 years old. It was MS-DOS running on an i386 machine. I started coding in middle school, working with (very old) languages such as BASIC and Pascal/Delphi. In high school, I discovered C/C++. In grad school, Python.

    Python has since become my primary language — I even published a book on it in 2014 (one of the first open-source Python solutions to classical computer science problems in algorithms and data structures).

    Throughout my career, I've had the opportunity to write production code in many other languages, including Ruby (Shopify), Swift and Objective-C (Apple), Go, C++, Java, Rust, Assembly, Solidity, Shell, Terraform, CUDA, IDL, MATLAB, R, and JavaScript.

    These days, I'm super fan of Lean, I build AI and backends in Python and Rust, and I vibe-code in TypeScript.

  • What's your story with Linux?

    I discovered and learned Linux back in high school, when I was studying at ET-UFPR, mostly through a magazine called Revista do Linux (or Linux Brasil). At the time, I was using a Brazilian distribution called Conectiva Linux — and I eventually ended up interning at the company for a little while.

    In college, I first spent two years studying engineering at POLI-USP and then completed a bachelor's degree in Physics. During that time, I experimented with a bunch of different distributions, mostly with dual- or multi- boot with GRUB, from the hardcore ones, like Gentoo and Slackware, to the almost-too-easy ones, like Ubuntu and Mint. I eventually settled on Fedora and Debian as my favorites. They were different enough that I enjoyed having two machines, each running one of them.

    During my PhD, I used mostly Fedora, as well as Red Hat and actual UNIX servers in the labs and on the large computing systems where I ran my research.

    When I became a software engineer, I joined Apple as a Senior Engineer in my first year. I gradually ended up alternating between periods when I did most of my work on Linux and periods where I worked primarily on macOS. I regret a little bit, and I hate that I ended up forming habits around it.

    For the last couple of years, I've been using two separate machines for work: my super-secure, secret, heavily protected coding and research station running Linux (I've been using Omarchy since 2025, because cool && mice_suck), and a honeypot box running macOS, where I read the news and articles, watch podcasts, open dubious links, and generally assume it could be compromised (I also rotate them a bunch — for instance, I've had five different MacBooks since 2024).

  • What's your story with videogames?

    When I was 5 or so, my dad brought an Atari home, and we spent the following nights playing and eating bowls of french fries (his specialty). Around 10, my mom bought us a Super Nintendo, which is still my favorite console of all time. After that, we had a Mega Drive (Sega Genesis), a Nintendo 64, PlayStation 1, 2, 3, 4, Xbox 360, Nintendo Wii, and the Meta VR series. Plus, I have always been a PC gamer at the same time.

    I have too many favorite games, but in terms of hours played, I would highlight all the old-school PC LAN games and the SNES and PS1 classics, with a sweet spot for Resident Evils, Silent Hills, Tomb Raiders, Half-Life/Portals, Left 4 Deads, Age of Empires, Fallouts, WoW, StarCrafts, and Diablos.

    P.S. I've always been a Linux girl, but I might have had to use Windows and macOS for my Steam and Blizzard games. Or not.

  • What's your story with traveling?

    During the first part of my life, I had a big dream of exploring the world and experiencing different cultures, so I took every opportunity to travel through my research.

    Over the last decade, my work as an engineer has paid reasonably well and allowed me to work remotely, which has enabled me to pursue this objective consistently.

    I’ve been to 30+ countries, lived in dozens of cities, and visited almost every state in the U.S. One of my goals for the second part of my life is to continue traveling with my family and telling beautiful, compelling stories about these experiences and the things I’ve seen.

  • What's your story with astronomy and astrology?

    I grew up absolutely fascinated by science and computers, and I could never decide which one I loved more.

    This became clear in college, when I started in (Electrical / Computer) Engineering, and switched to Physics halfway through. At the time, I read a bunch of Carl Sagan, Marcelo Gleiser, Brian Greene, Kip Thorne, Sean Carroll, Stephen Hawking, Steven Weinberg, Richard Feynman, Alan Guth, and the thinkers of the Copenhagen school, including Heisenberg and Bohr. There was no better subject to study, and I went to pursue a PhD.

    I did not believe in astrology at all (although, because of a peculiar autistic quirk of mine, I remember the birthdays of every person I have ever met). In fact, I once tried to design an experiment to debunk astrology. I asked my colleagues to fill out a questionnaire and planned to analyze the results statistically to demonstrate that their personalities could not be explained by their zodiac signs. Given that my only exposure to astrology at the time was through horoscopes and similar material, the conclusion seemed obvious.

    During my five years pursuing a PhD in Theoretical Physics at Stony Brook, I was fortunate to explore many of the questions about physics that fascinated me at both extremes: the very large — the universe — and the very small — elementary particles. I have continued to think about these questions throughout my life. Now, with the emergence of AI, I expect to see many of them finally answered, and I am excited about what the future holds.

    My relationship with astrology, however, took a rather unexpected turn. In 2020, a PhD colleague of mine from Zapata Computing introduced me to birth charts. He was a very intelligent person, so I decided to investigate more. As I tend to do with anything that captures my interest, I went down the rabbit hole: I read dozens of books and analyzed hundreds of birth charts, all while applying the skeptical, scientific mindset I have used throughout my life.

    I began noticing correlations and patterns, particularly in theories involving ascendants and angles. There are still several open questions that I cannot confidently answer — and that I also cannot simply dismiss. Astrology has therefore become one of the subjects I continue to study seriously throughout my life, alongside computers, physics, math, philosophy, literature, movies, art, and music. Just like Isaac Newton.

  • What's your story with bitcoin?

    It's not very fashionable to say this today, but we are all Satoshi. All I can say is that I've been using the handle bytegirl (bt3gl) since 1998. And, cute enough, bitcoin.org was registered on August 18, while its seminal paper came out on the best holiday of the year.

    Back in 2018, I was already indoctrinated in the philosophies of decentralization. My mom had completed a master's thesis on financial decentralization in Brazilian schools, and I was reading a lot of anarchist and libertarian texts. On top of that, I've always found the mathematics of cryptography beautiful, as well as questions like how quantum computers might eventually break existing cryptographic systems.

    I returned to these ideas after leaving my amazing engineering role at Shopify at the end of 2021 to co-found a decentralized storytelling company (DAO) with a few friends (Storyteller NFT; Midsummer; Story Protocol). The venture was short-lived, but I ended up staying in the space for a few years, working on problems involving fair arbitrage, game theory, privacy, and cryptography.

    As of 2026, I've returned to the AI/ML space, and this is where I'll be building for the next decades. Although I still believe in the ethos of decentralization, I cannot endorse how toxic and casino-like things have become — but I do hope this will change.

  • What's your story with religions?

    My family is German and Italian, with a Jewish side, but I was raised Catholic (and I am confirmed). In my 20s, I became atheist. In my 30s, I explored several aspects of spirituality, including Eastern philosophies (and I was vegetarian for many years).

    Nowadays, although I am drawn to the Jewish and Christian traditions, my views of reality remain highly eclectic (as you can see from some of my artistic side projects).

    In a practical sense, I believe I've found the personal answers I needed. I live a peaceful but stoic and self-disciplined life, devoted to my craft and the people I love. I read a lot of philosophy and poetry, and I look at the flowers and the sky with awe.
