  • What's your story with Linux?

    I discovered and learned Linux back in high school, when I was studying at ET-UFPR, mostly through a magazine called Revista do Linux (or Linux Brasil). At the time, I was using a Brazilian distribution called Conectiva Linux — and I eventually ended up interning at the company for a little while.

    In college, I first spent two years studying engineering at POLI-USP and then completed a bachelor's degree in Physics. During that time, I experimented with a bunch of different distributions, mostly with dual- or multi- booting with GRUB, from the hardcore ones, like Gentoo and Slackware, to the almost-too-easy ones, like Ubuntu and Mint. I eventually settled on Fedora and Debian as my favorites. They were different enough that I enjoyed having two machines, each running one of them.

    During my PhD, I used mostly Fedora, as well as Red Hat and actual UNIX servers in the labs and on the large computing systems where I ran my research. When I became a software engineer, I joined Apple as a Senior Engineer in my first year, and I experimented with macOS for a while.

    Nowadays, all my machines run Linux (and I've been using Omarchy since 2025, together with another private setup). My mobile devices are either disposable or run GrapheneOS and LineageOS.

  • What's your story with astronomy and astrology?

    I grew up absolutely fascinated by science and computers, and I could never decide which one I loved more.

    This became clear in college, when I started in (Electrical / Computer) Engineering, and switched to Physics halfway through. At the time, I read a bunch of Carl Sagan, Marcelo Gleiser, Brian Greene, Kip Thorne, Sean Carroll, Stephen Hawking, Steven Weinberg, Richard Feynman, Alan Guth, and the thinkers of the Copenhagen school, including Heisenberg and Bohr. There was no better subject to study, and I pursued a PhD.

    I did not believe in astrology at all (although, because of a peculiar autistic quirk of mine, I remember the birthdays of every person I have ever met). In fact, I once tried to design an experiment to debunk astrology. I asked my colleagues to fill out a questionnaire and planned to analyze the results statistically to demonstrate that their personalities could not be explained by their zodiac signs. Given that my only exposure to astrology at the time was through horoscopes and similar material, the conclusion seemed obvious.

    During my five years pursuing a PhD in Theoretical Physics at Stony Brook, I was fortunate to explore many of the questions about physics that fascinated me at both extremes: the very large — the universe — and the very small — elementary particles. I have continued to think about these questions throughout my life. Now, with the emergence of AI, I expect to see many of them finally answered, and I am excited about what the future holds.

    My relationship with astrology, however, took a rather unexpected turn. In 2020, a PhD colleague of mine from Zapata Computing introduced me to birth charts. He was a very intelligent person, so I decided to investigate more. As I tend to do with anything that captures my interest, I went down the rabbit hole: I read dozens of books and analyzed hundreds of birth charts, all while applying the skeptical, scientific mindset I have used throughout my life.

    I began to notice correlations and patterns, particularly in theories involving ascendants and angles, and I have been creating a mathematical theory drawing on my knowledge of Group Theory and other related fields. There are still several open questions that I cannot confidently answer — and that I also cannot simply dismiss.

    Astrology has therefore become one of the subjects I will continue to study seriously throughout my life, alongside astronomy, computer science, physics, math, philosophy, literature, history, movies, art, and music. Just like Isaac Newton.

    Yes, I am. But not your usual snobby academic, full of peer-reviewed publications and diplomas on the wall. I am the real, hardcore, Newtonian, Bohrian, Einsteinian, genius-like inventor and scientist. I am the one who will do what is risky but logical instead of what looks good on the gram.

Then, I went straight from undergraduate studies to a PhD in Physics at Stony Brook University, one of the most well-regarded places in the world for String Theory. Therefore, I've spent nearly a decade of my life studying theoretical physics full-time.

    During those years, between São Paulo and New York, I survived on scholarships. I had full tuition coverage, teaching assistantships, and research assistantships, which meant that I spent the first five years of my life in the USA, by myself and without any family support, living on a $27k/year scholarship.

    I wanted to learn every single subject possible, so I took roughly 1.5 times as many classes as I needed for my PhD, including several in mathematics and computer science, and I took them for the only reason that has ever mattered to me: learning. It was not exactly the easiest thing to do, because the workload of a PhD is brutal. But I did it because I always do more than is required. Because I am an overachiever.

    That meant that for nearly a decade, I chose to learn science under very raw living conditions. While everyone else was getting good jobs and beginning their normal adult lives, I took the road less traveled. I did hardcore mode.

    I spent most of those years in an office without windows, doing pages and pages of calculations involving integrals, Lagrangians, and Hamiltonians. I had enough money for two meals a day, and I lived in a house with many other graduate students. For many years, that was my life.

My attitude was very passive: I would simply try to ignore it and continue focusing on my work. It seems like certain things never change.

I turned it down for a position as a software engineer in the Bay Area at Yelp, making $130k/year, with the chance to finally have my own place, buy cool clothes, get a cool car, and start my life outside school. Plus, I had already learned what I wanted from my PhD. Now it was time to learn how to build distributed systems and conquer California. I was 30 years old.

    From 2014 to the present — another decade — I have devoted myself fully to software engineering, while continuing studies in philosophy, art, languages, and cultures. Now, during the third decade of my career, I am putting everything together to build what comes next in coordinated superintelligence. I cannot think of anyone more qualified than I am to do this.

    If you admire the scientists and engineers who give their lives to their craft, then the next time we meet, be respectful — and call me Dr. von Steinkirch. I would like to meet anyone who would dare, at this point in humanity, considering my work ethic, my dedication, and my struggles, to tell me otherwise.
